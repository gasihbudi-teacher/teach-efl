# Adjective Clause vs Adjective Phrase

## Lesson overview

**Audience:** Grades 10–12  
**Suggested time:** 90 minutes  
**Focus:** Recognising adjective clauses and adjectival phrases, then using selected patterns to make descriptions more concise.

### Learning objectives

By the end of the lesson, students should be able to:

1. Recognise the noun being described.
2. Distinguish a finite adjective clause from a phrase that modifies a noun.
3. Recognise four useful patterns: present participle, past participle, adjective phrase, and prepositional phrase.
4. Reduce selected adjective clauses when the grammar and meaning allow it.
5. Use the structures in their own sentences.

### Suggested lesson flow

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–8 min | Warm-up: compare two descriptions |
| 8–18 min | Notice the difference: clause or phrase? |
| 18–48 min | Teach selected patterns and guided completions |
| 48–62 min | Quick-Fire replacement |
| 62–80 min | Independent practice and explanation |
| 80–90 min | Review and readiness check |

The teacher does not need to use all forty starters in one meeting. Select the items that fit the learners' readiness and available time.

## Start with a familiar situation

Imagine two people are trying to find a friend in a busy place.

**Person A:** The girl **who is wearing a red jacket** is my friend.

**Person B:** The girl **wearing a red jacket** is my friend.

Both descriptions help us identify the same girl.

Ask learners:

- Which girl are we talking about?
- What information helps us identify her?
- What changes between the two descriptions?

Invite learners to read both sentences aloud. Do not begin with a long grammar definition. Let them notice that both highlighted parts describe the noun **girl**.

### The simple difference

**Adjective clause:** a group of words that describes a noun and contains its own subject and finite verb.

Example: the girl **who is wearing a red jacket**

- Subject: who
- Finite verb: is wearing
- Noun described: girl

**Adjectival phrase:** a group of words that describes a noun but does not contain its own subject and finite verb.

Example: the girl **wearing a red jacket**

- Describing structure: wearing a red jacket
- No separate subject and finite verb
- Noun described: girl

Both answer a question such as: **Which person or thing?**

## Teaching stages

### Stage 1 — Present participle phrase (-ing)

#### Notice the pattern

**Adjective clause**

The woman **who is carrying a shopping bag** is my neighbour.

**Adjectival phrase**

The woman **carrying a shopping bag** is my neighbour.

Both sentences describe the woman. The second description is shorter.

#### Pattern

- Adjective clause: noun + who/which/that + be + V-ing
- Adjectival phrase: noun + V-ing phrase

#### How it works

When the adjective clause has the pattern **relative pronoun + be + V-ing**, we can often remove the relative pronoun and the form of **be**. Keep the V-ing form.

**Try saying it aloud:**

“The woman who is carrying a shopping bag…”  
“The woman carrying a shopping bag…”

### Stage 2 — Past participle phrase (V3)

#### Notice the pattern

**Adjective clause**

The song **that was recorded at home** became popular.

**Adjectival phrase**

The song **recorded at home** became popular.

Both sentences describe the song. The phrase **recorded at home** gives information about the song.

#### Pattern

- Adjective clause: noun + who/which/that + be + V3
- Adjectival phrase: noun + V3 phrase

#### How it works

This pattern commonly describes a noun that receives an action (passive meaning).

When the adjective clause has the pattern **relative pronoun + be + V3**, we can often remove the relative pronoun and **be**, then keep the past participle.

#### Your turn

Complete each second sentence.

1. The cake that was made by my aunt smells delicious.  
   The cake __________ smells delicious.

2. The photograph that was taken yesterday looks beautiful.  
   The photograph __________ looks beautiful.

   The message __________ is important.

4. The shoes that were designed for running are comfortable.  
   The shoes __________ are comfortable.

5. The song that was recorded at home became popular.  
   The song __________ became popular.

6. The phone that was repaired by a technician works perfectly.  
   The phone __________ works perfectly.

7. The documentary that was produced by local filmmakers received positive reviews.  
   The documentary __________ received positive reviews.

8. The package that was delivered to the wrong address has not arrived.  
   The package __________ has not arrived.

9. The jacket that was damaged during the trip needs to be replaced.  
   The jacket __________ needs to be replaced.

10. The application that was developed by a small team has attracted many users.  
    The application __________ has attracted many users.

### Stage 3 — Adjective phrase

#### Notice the pattern

**Adjective clause**

The singer **who is famous for her powerful voice** has many fans.

**Adjective phrase**

The singer **famous for her powerful voice** has many fans.

The phrase **famous for her powerful voice** describes the singer.

#### Pattern

- Adjective clause: noun + who/which/that + be + adjective phrase
- Adjective phrase: noun + adjective phrase

#### How it works

When a relative clause uses **be + adjective phrase**, the relative pronoun and **be** can sometimes be omitted when the adjective phrase follows the noun.

This pattern is especially useful with adjectives that have complements, such as:

- afraid of dogs
- famous for her voice
- popular with teenagers
- proud of her work
- responsible for the event
- aware of other people's boundaries

#### Your turn

Complete each second sentence.

   The boy __________ stays away from them.

3. The singer who is famous for her beautiful voice has many fans.  
   The singer __________ has many fans.

4. The café that is popular with teenagers is always crowded.  
   The café __________ is always crowded.

5. The phone that is useful for travelling is quite expensive.  
   The phone __________ is quite expensive.

6. The fans who are excited about the concert are waiting outside.

7. The creator who is proud of her latest video shared it online.  
   The creator __________ shared it online.

8. The film that is suitable for younger viewers received good reviews.  
   The film __________ received good reviews.

9. The people who are responsible for the event have arrived.  
   The people __________ have arrived.

10. The friend who is aware of other people's boundaries is someone we can trust.  
    The friend __________ is someone we can trust.

### Stage 4 — Prepositional phrase functioning as a noun modifier

#### Notice the pattern

**Adjective clause**

The keys **that are on the table** belong to my mother.

**Prepositional phrase**

The keys **on the table** belong to my mother.

Both descriptions identify the keys. The words **on the table** tell us which keys.

#### Pattern

- Adjective clause: noun + who/which/that + be + prepositional phrase
- Prepositional phrase: noun + prepositional phrase

#### How it works

A prepositional phrase can follow a noun and describe its location, position, or relationship to something else.

When an adjective clause uses **relative pronoun + be + prepositional phrase**, we can often remove the relative pronoun and **be**.

## Quick-Fire replacement

**Purpose:** Keep the target structure and the logical relationship, but replace selected information. Students become more independent as the highlighting is reduced.

### Starting model

**Adjective clause:** The woman who is carrying a **red** umbrella is my neighbour.

**Adjectival phrase:** The woman carrying a **red** umbrella is my neighbour.

### Round 1 — Word replacement

Replace one highlighted word. Keep the rest of the sentence.

- Original: carrying a **red** umbrella
- New word: **yellow**

Possible responses:

- The woman who is carrying a yellow umbrella is my neighbour.
- The woman carrying a yellow umbrella is my neighbour.

### Round 2 — Phrase replacement

Replace the highlighted phrase. Adjust the verb if needed.

- Original: carrying a yellow umbrella
- New phrase: **holding two shopping bags**

Possible responses:

- The woman who is holding two shopping bags is my neighbour.
- The woman holding two shopping bags is my neighbour.

### Round 3 — Clause or idea replacement

Replace the whole additional idea. Give less visual support.

- New idea: **waiting for the bus**

Possible responses:

- The woman who is waiting for the bus is my neighbour.
- The woman waiting for the bus is my neighbour.

### Teacher prompts

- “Which word will you change?”
- “Does the new phrase still describe the woman?”
- “Do you need to change the verb?”
- “Can you make a new description without copying the model?”

Accept different answers if they are grammatical and logically relevant. Check verb form, agreement, pronoun reference, and meaning.

## Common mistakes

### Mistake 1 — Thinking every adjective clause can be reduced

Not every adjective clause can be reduced using the patterns in this lesson. Check the structure and meaning before removing words.

### Mistake 2 — Thinking an omitted relative pronoun always creates a phrase

Compare:

- The woman **who I met yesterday** is friendly.
- The woman **I met yesterday** is friendly.

The second sentence still contains an adjective clause. The relative pronoun **who** is omitted because it is the object of **met**.

The clause still has:

- Subject: I
- Finite verb: met

So it is not an adjectival phrase.

### Mistake 3 — Thinking every -ing word creates an adjective phrase

Look at the job the words do in the sentence. A word ending in **-ing** can appear in different grammatical structures. Do not classify a structure by its spelling alone.

### Mistake 4 — Treating every V3 form as passive

A past participle can describe a noun, but the meaning and structure still need to be checked in context.

## Final understanding check

Classify each bold part as an **adjective clause** or an **adjectival phrase**. For each answer, identify the noun being described.

1. The man **who lives nearby** is a chef.
2. The bag **under the chair** is mine.
3. The person **I called yesterday** is busy.
4. The jacket **damaged during the trip** belongs to Sarah.
5. The people **interested in photography** joined the discussion.

<details>
<summary>Answers and short reasons</summary>

1. **Adjective clause** — describes *man*. It has the subject **who** and the finite verb **lives**.
2. **Prepositional phrase functioning as an adjectival modifier** — describes *bag*. It has no separate subject and finite verb.
3. **Adjective clause** — describes *person*. The relative pronoun is omitted, but the clause still has the subject **I** and finite verb **called**.
4. **Past participial phrase functioning as an adjectival modifier** — describes *jacket*.
5. **Adjective phrase** — describes *people*. The adjective **interested** has the complement **in photography**.

</details>

## Readiness checkpoint

The learner is ready to move forward when they can:

- Point to the noun being described.
- Find the subject and finite verb in a finite adjective clause.
- Recognise a phrase that modifies a noun.
- Complete selected reductions while keeping the intended meaning.
- Explain why an omitted relative pronoun does not always make a phrase.

If the learner gets stuck:

1. Return to the complete model.
2. Point out the noun being described.
3. Offer two vocabulary choices.
4. Ask the learner to choose one and complete the description.
5. Try a new example with less support.

The goal is accurate, meaningful use—not finishing all ten items in every pattern.

## Teacher note: terminology

This lesson uses **adjectival phrase** as a broad functional label for a phrase that modifies a noun. The four stages include participial phrases, adjective phrases, and prepositional phrases functioning as noun modifiers.

Some grammar books use **adjective phrase** more narrowly for a phrase headed by an adjective. If the student's school textbook distinguishes phrase type from grammatical function, explain that distinction when marking the work.

The reduction patterns in this lesson are selected useful patterns, not a rule that every adjective clause can be shortened.
